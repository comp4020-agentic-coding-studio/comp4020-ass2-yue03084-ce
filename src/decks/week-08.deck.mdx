# Nap pods and sleep as a product

The need becomes a purchase

---

## Last week, in one line

Who is permitted to rest.

This week: the answer industry arrived at --- **buy something**.

---

## What the pod is for

It makes daytime sleep **legible to an organisation**.

- sleeping at a desk is ambiguous
- sleeping in a designated capsule is authorised

Much of the product is that authorisation. The furniture is its delivery
mechanism.

---

## The business case, honestly stated

Vendors sell alertness, error reduction, retention.

The underlying research is **real**. It is also:

- mostly small
- mostly short-term
- mostly not conducted in the settings being sold to

---

## What a purchase avoids

| Option | Costs | Touches control |
|---|---|---|
| A pod | money **once** | no |
| Shorter shifts | money **continuously** | yes |
| Later starts | money continuously | yes |
| Lower monitoring | money continuously | yes |

Given a choice between an object and a change in schedule, institutions buy the
object.

# Four hundred staff, two pods

The arithmetic is the policy

---

## Whatever the announcement says

Two capsules cannot hold four hundred people's afternoons.

They were never meant to. They were meant to be **visible**.

This is the week's central observation: the object settles a political question
quietly, and in the buyer's favour.

---

## Rest as a consumer market

Sleep apps. Weighted blankets. Soundscapes. Tracked sleep scores.

Note the shift in **who is responsible**.

Rest becomes a personal project you may be failing at, rather than a condition
your employer arranges.

---

## The exception worth taking seriously

Aviation, and some clinical settings, have rostered rest that genuinely changed
practice.

What was different: **a regulator required it.**

Nobody bought their way there.

## Tell me the pod I am being unfair to

> The nap pod is not the nap returning. It is the nap being made purchasable, so
> that the schedule does not have to change.
