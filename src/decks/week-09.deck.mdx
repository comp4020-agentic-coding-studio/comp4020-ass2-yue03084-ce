# Infants, elders, shift workers

The people for whom the nap is least optional

---

## Until now, in one line

The course has mostly concerned **healthy adults on a standard day**.

That was a convenience. This week it goes.

---

## Three groups, one pattern

| Group | The claim | The access |
|---|---|---|
| Infants and children | polyphasic by default | provided, then withdrawn |
| Older adults | nighttime sleep fragments | read as decline |
| **Shift workers** | **measurable occupational hazard** | **frequently prohibited** |

Strength of claim does not predict permission.

---

## Infants and children

- polyphasic by default, consolidating over years
- naps here are uncontroversial and institutionally provided
- provision stops at an age set by **school timetables**

Not by anything developmental.

---

## Older adults

Nighttime sleep fragments with age, and daytime sleep often returns.

The interesting part is the interpretation: the same behaviour that is
developmentally normal in a child is read as **decline** in an older person.

Sometimes medicalised.

---

## Shift workers

The clearest case in the course.

- circadian misalignment is a measurable occupational hazard
- documented consequences for error rates and long-term health
- rostered napping demonstrably helps

---

{/* _class: impact */}

# Strongest claim, least access

The group with the best evidence is the group most often forbidden

---

## Why

Safety-critical industries frequently prohibit on-shift sleep outright.

Week 7's explanation applies directly, unchanged:

The objection is to **how it looks**, not to what it does.

---

## Where it was fixed, and how

Aviation is the case worth studying.

Controlled rest on the flight deck was **regulated into existence**, against
industry resistance.

Not bought. Not encouraged. Required.

---

## Who is missing from this list

- carers
- people with chronic illness or disability
- anyone whose fatigue is contested rather than assumed

Strong claim, no access — and not yet a policy question anyone has taken up.

## Tell me which group you'd argue for

> Across all three groups, permission to sleep in the day correlates with social
> position and not with physiological need. The nap pod cannot fix this, because
> it was never the kind of problem a purchase addresses.
