I am a labour historian by training, and I arrived at this subject from the
wrong end. I was reading nineteenth-century factory rules for a project about
timekeeping, noticed how much of the disciplinary language was about attention
and wakefulness, and could not find anyone who had written about what happened
to the afternoon sleep those rules were displacing. The answer turned out to
need a sleep scientist, an anthropologist and a public health researcher, which
is roughly what this course is.

What I teach here is weeks 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11 and 12 — the historical and
institutional spine. The claim I am most attached to, and the one I would like
you to attack hardest, is that permission to rest during the day tracks social
position rather than physiological need. I think the evidence for it is strong.
I also think I am the wrong person to notice if it is weaker than I believe, so
week 7's lab is deliberately built to let a room of students find the holes.

## What to come to me about

Course structure, assessment briefs, extensions, and anything where you are not
sure who to ask. If I am the wrong person I will say so and tell you who is.

If you are in the first category of student this course attracts — the one that
signed up because the title was funny — you are genuinely welcome, and I would
ask only that you decide by about week 5 whether you are staying. That is the
week the course turns, and it is much harder to catch up on the argument than
on the reading.

## Consultation

After each Nap Lab, in the same room, for as long as anyone is still asking
things. This is usually the most useful half hour of my week. If that time does
not work for you, email me and we will find another — several of you are
shift workers, which this course of all courses should manage to accommodate.
