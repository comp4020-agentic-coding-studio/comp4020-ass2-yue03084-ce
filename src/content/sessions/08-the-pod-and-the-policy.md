This lab is arithmetic, and it is the most directly useful session of the
semester for your final assessment. You will cost two ways of giving people
rest, discover that the cheaper one is the one nobody adopts, and have to
explain that.

## Before the lab

- Bring the workplace map from last week and the advertised product you were
  asked to find.
- Bring rough numbers: headcount, shift pattern, whether the work is monitored,
  whether anyone can close a door. Estimates are fine. Say they are estimates.

## In the lab

**Two costings, forty minutes.** For the same workplace, in pairs:

1. **The purchase.** Pods or a rest room. Units, floor space taken from
   something else, cleaning, scheduling, the number of people served per hour.
   The last of those is the one that ends most arguments.
2. **The schedule.** A rostered rest period. Lost hours, cover, overtime, and
   who has to agree to it.

**Read the vendor's evidence, twenty minutes.** We take one piece of real
marketing material and follow the citations. Some of them go somewhere. The
exercise is to say precisely where the claim outruns the study — sample,
setting, duration, or measure — rather than to conclude that it is all
nonsense, which is lazy and also wrong.

**Which one gets adopted, twenty minutes.** Each pair states which intervention
their institution would actually choose, and then the harder part: whether that
is the same as the one they would defend. Very few rooms find these are the same
answer, and the gap between them is precisely the thing your final proposal has
to be honest about.

## Afterwards

Your [Cultural Case Study](/assessments/) is due this week. Submit the argument
you settled on, not the material you gathered.

Keep both costings. The [Nap Policy Proposal](/assessments/) asks for a defence,
not a wish, and a proposal that has priced its own recommendation is immediately
in a different category from one that has not. If today's numbers said your
preferred intervention is unaffordable, that is not a setback — a proposal that
argues for the expensive option knowing the price is far stronger than one that
never checked.
