The last Nap Lab, and the only one where the thing being measured is what you
remember rather than how you feel. It also closes a loop the course opened in
week 1, because the instruments you will use are the ones you were taught then.

## Before the lab

- Sleep normally the night before. A tired room produces a bigger effect and a
  worthless one, since the comparison we want is not against sleep deprivation.
- Bring your Nap Log. Ten weeks of it is about to become useful in an unexpected
  way.

## In the lab

**Learn, then split, then recall.** The room learns a set of material under
controlled conditions. Half then nap; half stay awake doing something
undemanding. Both are tested at the same delay, so the only difference between
the groups is what happened in the gap.

**You will want to be in the nap group.** Assignment is random, and the wanting
is worth noticing: the expectation effect is exactly what makes self-reported
study advice unreliable, and you are currently generating some.

**The pooled result, and what it cannot tell us, twenty minutes.** We put both
groups' recall on the board. One room, one session, one task — this cannot
establish the effect and is not trying to. What it can do is show you the size
of the noise, which is the thing that makes published effect sizes legible.

**Reading the actual study, twenty-five minutes.** We take one well-known
consolidation paper and read the method rather than the abstract. How many
participants, what task, what control, how long the delay, and what exactly was
measured. Almost every over-claim you will meet about sleep and learning is
visible at this level of reading, which is the transferable skill of the
session.

**Your exam period, last ten minutes.** Individually: one thing you will do
differently, and one observation that would tell you whether it helped. This is
the Karolinska-and-reaction-time habit from week 1, pointed at yourself one last
time.
