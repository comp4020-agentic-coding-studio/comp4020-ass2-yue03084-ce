A nap policy for one real institution, and its defence. This is the largest
piece of work in the course and it has been assembled across four sessions
rather than set at the end: the map from week 7, the costings from week 8, the
named beneficiary from week 9, and the objections from week 12.

Around 2000 words, plus whatever the costing needs.

## What a policy has to contain

**A real institution.** One you can describe in specifics — headcount, shift
pattern, what is monitored, who can close a door. A proposal for a generic
office is the most common way this assessment fails, because every sentence in
it has to stay vague enough to be true of all offices, and a policy that vague
cannot be evaluated or objected to.

**A named beneficiary.** Not "staff". Which staff, on which shift, with what
currently available to them. You settled this in week 9.

**An actual change.** To the schedule, the space, or the rules. Week 12 named
the two failure modes and they are both here in force:

- *The wish* — a statement of institutional support with nothing behind it. No
  time, no space, no cover, nobody accountable.
- *The purchase* — equipment bought so the schedule need not change. Week 8
  costed this against the alternative, and if you propose a purchase you must
  say why it beats the schedule change at the same price.

**A cost, and what it displaces.** Money, hours, floor space, cover. Against at
least one alternative use of the same resources. A proposal that argues for the
expensive option *knowing the price* is strong; one that never checked is not.

**A measure.** How would anyone know whether this worked? Name the instrument.
After week 10 you cannot answer "we'll give everyone a sleep tracker" without
addressing what that device can and cannot detect — a {NAP_MINUTES}-minute nap
is at or below its resolution, and you now know it.

## The defence is the biggest single component

Weighted at 35%, because a policy nobody has argued against is untested.

Take the objections seriously and in their strongest form. The four from week
12 — cost, fairness, appearances, precedent — are the ones that get raised in
practice, and the fairness objection ("if they can, everyone will want to") is
the one that has killed the most real policies. Answer the version an
intelligent opponent would make, not the weak version you can dismiss.

**Name the objection you could not fully answer.** This is worth marks rather
than costing them. A proposal that concedes a real limitation is more credible
than one that pretends there is none, and every marker on this course can tell
the difference between a conceded weakness and an overlooked one.

## Grounding

Use the course. The afternoon trough is scheduled and varies by chronotype, so
a single fixed hour serves some people and inconveniences the rest. Permission
tracks position rather than need, so a policy that relies on informal discretion
will reproduce the hierarchy it is meant to fix. And aviation is the one
unambiguous success in the record: evidence, then a regulator, then a
requirement. A proposal resting on goodwill has assumed away the hard part, and
week 9 is where you learnt why.

## Format

A policy document, not an essay. Headings, a stated scope, the actual
provisions, the costing, and the defence. Write it so the person who would have
to approve it could read it — which, in week 12, somebody played.
