Fourteen days of your own sleep and alertness, and an analysis of it. The
recording starts after the week 2 Nap Lab and the format is the one you settled
in week 1.

Read the weighting before you start: the record is worth less than the analysis
of it. A meticulous fortnight of entries with three sentences of summary is the
most common way this assessment goes wrong, and it is a hard one to recover
from because the recording period cannot be repeated.

## What to record

Every day, including days with no nap — absences are data, and a log with the
empty days quietly dropped cannot support any claim about frequency.

For each day:

- **Both measures from week 1**: a Karolinska reading and a reaction-time
  median, taken the same way each time.
- **If you napped**: lights-down time, duration, and both measures taken within
  two minutes of waking and again at fifteen.
- **Context worth one line**: caffeine and when, previous night's sleep, and
  anything unusual. You are not writing a diary; you are recording the variables
  you might later need to explain a strange number.
- **Your uncertainty.** "Unsure whether I slept" is a real and frequent
  observation. Record it rather than resolving it — week 10 is about how badly
  the available instruments resolve exactly this, and your own uncertainty is
  part of that argument.

The lab entries from weeks 2, 3 and 4 belong in here too. That is three of your
fourteen days already structured, including the {NAP_MINUTES}-minute nap and the
coffee-nap comparison.

## The analysis

Around 800 words. Not a summary of the log — an argument from it.

A strong analysis does four things:

1. **States a finding.** Something specific enough to be wrong. "My trough is
   about ninety minutes earlier than the lecture's typical case" is a finding;
   "I learned a lot about my sleep" is not.
2. **Supports it from your own numbers.** Point at the entries. If the claim
   rests on four days out of fourteen, say so.
3. **Says what the data cannot show.** You have one participant, no control
   condition, and full knowledge of what the course predicts. Name the specific
   way that could have produced your result, not the generic caveat.
4. **Reports what went against you.** The week 4 prediction you sealed in week 1
   is the obvious candidate. If it was wrong, say so and say by how much.

## How it is marked

Analysis carries the most weight, and honesty about limitations carries real
weight of its own — that is deliberate. A clean, modest finding with its
weaknesses named scores above a confident one that ignores them.

The commonest ways to lose marks here, in order: no analysis to speak of; a
finding the log does not actually support; dropping the days that did not fit;
and describing the limitations in the abstract rather than saying which of them
applies to your fortnight.
