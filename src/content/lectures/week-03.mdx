Last week established that a short nap works because it stops before slow-wave
sleep. This week is about the one intervention that makes it work harder, and
about why it sounds like folklore when it is closer to a timetable.

## Outline

- **Adenosine.** It accumulates in the brain across the waking day and binds to
  receptors that make you feel sleepy. Sleep clears it. This is the closest
  thing we have to a physical substance for "tiredness".
- **What caffeine actually does.** It does not add energy. It is shaped enough
  like adenosine to occupy the same receptors without activating them, so the
  adenosine already present cannot be felt. The tiredness is still there and is
  still accruing.
- **The arrival time.** Caffeine taken by mouth takes roughly twenty to thirty
  minutes to reach useful concentration. This is the number the whole lecture
  turns on.
- **The coincidence.** A nap of about {NAP_MINUTES} minutes clears some
  adenosine, and the caffeine arrives to occupy the receptors just as you wake.
  Drink first, then sleep — the order is the intervention.
- **Why the evidence is thinner than the internet suggests.** The studies are
  small, mostly on young adults, mostly on simulated driving. The effect looks
  real. The size of it does not survive the retelling.

## The claim to argue with

> A coffee nap beats a nap and beats a coffee, and the reason is arithmetic
> about two timelines rather than anything about coffee.

If you do not drink caffeine, you are not excused from this argument. You are
the control condition, and this week's lab needs you.
