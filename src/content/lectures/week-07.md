Week 6 ended on a question: what does a person have to be seen to be, in order
to be allowed to sleep? This lecture takes that question out of other people's
cultures and puts it where it is harder to look at, which is the room you work
in.

## Outline

- **Autonomy tracks seniority.** The people most able to close a door are the
  people whose output is judged over months. The people least able are judged
  by the hour, or by a queue. This is the strongest single predictor of who can
  rest, and it is not about how tired anyone is.
- **Visibility is the mechanism.** The rule is rarely "no sleeping". It is that
  being *seen* asleep means something about you — and what it means depends
  entirely on who is doing the seeing and what they already assume.
- **Inemuri, revisited.** Last week's case is the cleanest illustration: the
  same act reads as devotion or as delinquency depending on the sleeper's
  position. The behaviour is constant, the interpretation is not.
- **Surveillance.** Monitored work removes the ambiguity that made informal rest
  possible. A break that is not logged is not taken, and the discretion that let
  people manage their own trough disappears without any rule being written.
- **The night shift.** Where napping is most defensible on safety grounds, it is
  most often forbidden. Week 9 takes this up properly; note here that the
  argument against it is about appearances rather than outcomes.
- **Who is believed to be tired.** Fatigue is not read the same way on everyone.
  Track who has to prove it and who is taken at their word.

## The claim to argue with

> Nap policy is not a wellbeing question. It is a question about who is trusted
> to manage their own time, and it produces the same answer as every other
> question of that shape.
