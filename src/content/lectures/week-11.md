Everything so far has treated the nap as recovery — something that undoes
tiredness. This week it does something else entirely: it processes what you
learned. This is the strongest experimental literature in the course, and the
most immediately useful to anyone sitting exams in a few weeks.

## Outline

- **Consolidation.** New memories are initially fragile and become durable over
  time. A good deal of that work happens during sleep, not merely in spite of
  it, and it can be observed as improvement without further practice.
- **Two mechanisms, roughly.** Slow-wave sleep is associated with stabilising
  declarative material — facts, events, things you could state. REM is
  associated with procedural skill and with the recombination that shows up as
  insight. A nap long enough to include both is doing two jobs.
- **Which recalls week 2.** Nap length decides which stages you get, so it
  decides which kind of consolidation you get. The twenty-versus-ninety choice
  returns here with a different reason behind it.
- **Sleep before learning.** A rested brain encodes better. Sleep deprivation
  impairs the *formation* of memories, not only their retrieval — so the
  all-nighter damages the material studied during it.
- **Sleep after learning.** Material learned and then slept on is retained
  better than material learned and then spent an equivalent waking period. A
  daytime nap produces a measurable version of this effect.
- **So the honest advice is both, and it is unwelcome.** Sleep before you study
  and after you study. The practice this displaces — studying instead of
  sleeping — is the one students reliably choose.
- **Reading the literature carefully.** Effect sizes here are modest, the tasks
  are laboratory tasks, and word-pair recall is not a degree. The direction of
  the finding is solid; the magnitude is routinely oversold.

## The claim to argue with

> A nap after studying is not a reward for studying. It is part of the studying,
> and skipping it costs more than the time it would have taken.
