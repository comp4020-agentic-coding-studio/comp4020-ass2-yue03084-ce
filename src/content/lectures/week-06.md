Last week argued that industrial work removed the nap. This week complicates
that, because it did not remove it everywhere, and the places that kept it did
not keep the same thing. Three regimes, and the differences between them are
more instructive than the fact that all three exist.

## Outline

- **Siesta.** A break in the working day itself, historically tied to heat and
  to a split shift. Note what it is: a gap in the schedule, taken at home,
  invisible to the employer. The Spanish version has been substantially eroded
  by the standard office day, which is itself evidence for week 5's argument.
- **午休 (wǔxiū).** The Chinese midday rest, institutionalised in schools and
  many workplaces — a scheduled, collective period, often at the desk. Written
  into the rhythm of the institution rather than left to the individual.
- **Inemuri.** Japanese sleeping-while-present: dozing in a meeting, on a train,
  at a desk. Not scheduled at all, and tolerated on a specific condition — that
  it reads as exhaustion from overwork. It is permitted because of what it
  signals, not despite it.
- **The axis that matters.** Not "do they nap?" but *where, with whom, and whose
  permission is implied*. Siesta is private and unscheduled by the employer;
  wǔxiū is collective and scheduled; inemuri is public and unscheduled.
- **Against the tourist reading.** Each of these is commonly described in
  English as a charming national quirk. Each is a labour arrangement, and each
  is contested within its own country.

## The claim to argue with

> The interesting question is never whether a culture naps. It is what a person
> has to be seen to be, in order to be allowed to.

That question is week 7's, and your Cultural Case Study will live or die on
whether you ask it.
