The nap is a biological need that modern work schedules erased. This course
studies its science, its history, and its return, and this lecture sets up the
first of those by answering a question most people think is already settled:
what a nap actually is.

## Outline

- **The course's claim.** Most adults feel a dip in alertness in the early
  afternoon, and most institutions are built as though they don't. Both halves
  of that sentence are worth a semester.
- **Sleep is a sequence, not a state.** Light sleep, then slow-wave sleep, then
  REM, and back around. Roughly ninety minutes for a full lap.
- **So a nap is not a small night's sleep.** It is an entry into that sequence,
  stopped partway. Which stage you were in when the alarm went is the single
  thing that decides how you feel afterwards — not how tired you were, and not
  whether you are "a nap person".
- **Why this is not obvious.** The experience of napping is a poor guide to it.
  People routinely cannot tell whether they slept, and are confident either way.
- **What we can measure, and how badly.** Self-reported sleepiness, reaction
  time, and — from week 10 — the devices that claim to do better than both.

## The claim to argue with

> Nobody is bad at napping. People are bad at choosing a length, and the length
> is not a matter of temperament.

You will get a proper chance to attack this next week, once you have the sleep
architecture to attack it with.
