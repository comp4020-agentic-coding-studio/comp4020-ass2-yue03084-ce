The nap is a biological need that modern work schedules erased. Twelve weeks
ago that was an assertion; by now it is the accumulated result of the science,
the history and the case studies. This lecture turns it into a design problem,
which is the only form in which it can be acted on.

## Outline

- **What the course established, in order.** Sleep is a sequence and nap length
  decides what you get from it. The afternoon trough is scheduled rather than
  caused by lunch. Industrial timekeeping removed the nap and attached shame to
  it. Other regimes kept it and permit different things. Permission tracks
  position, not need. Purchasing a pod avoids the schedule question. The
  populations with the strongest claim have the least access. The instruments
  most people would use cannot see a short nap. And sleep does not only restore
  — it consolidates.
- **What follows for design.** A policy has to survive three things: chronotype
  variation, so a single fixed hour fails; the visibility problem, so a
  permission nobody can use without being seen is not a permission; and
  measurement, so any promised benefit must be checkable with an instrument that
  can actually detect it.
- **The two failure modes.** The wish — a statement of support with nothing
  behind it, no space, no time, no cover. And the purchase — an object bought so
  that the schedule need not change. Week 8 costed both.
- **What worked, once.** Aviation. Evidence, then a regulator, then a
  requirement. Voluntary adoption on the strength of evidence alone is not in
  the record, and a proposal that assumes goodwill has assumed the hard part
  away.
- **Defending it.** Your proposal is assessed on its defence, and the defence is
  against a specific person with a specific objection — cost, fairness,
  appearances, or precedent. Anticipating the wrong objection is the most common
  way a good proposal fails.

## The claim to argue with

> Any nap policy that does not change the schedule, name who it is for, and say
> how it will be checked is a statement of support, not a policy. Most published
> ones are statements of support.

## After the course

The [Nap Policy Proposal](/assessments/) is due at the end of this week. Write
it for the institution you have been mapping since week 7, for the beneficiary
you named in week 9, at the price you established in week 8.

And then, if the argument of this course has held: go and have a nap. You now
know exactly how long it should be and precisely who would object.
