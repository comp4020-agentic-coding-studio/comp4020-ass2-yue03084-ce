For four weeks this course has argued that the nap is a biological need. This
week asks the obvious follow-up: if it is a need, what happened to it? The
answer is not that people stopped wanting to sleep in the afternoon. It is that
a particular way of organising work made it impossible, and then made it
shameful.

## Outline

- **Task time and clock time.** Before industrial work, the day was organised
  around tasks that had their own natural length. Afterwards it was organised
  around hours that did not care what you were doing. E. P. Thompson's account
  of that shift is the spine of this lecture.
- **The factory needs everyone at once.** A machine-paced production line only
  works if the whole shift is present simultaneously. Individual rest becomes
  not merely unproductive but disruptive — a structural fact, not a moral one.
- **Segmented sleep.** Roger Ekirch's evidence that pre-industrial Europeans
  commonly slept in two nighttime blocks with a waking interval between them.
  The consolidated eight-hour night is a recent arrangement, not a natural one.
- **Artificial light.** Cheap lighting pushed bedtime later without moving the
  start of work, and the compression had to come out of somewhere.
- **The moral turn.** This is the part worth the most attention. Sleeping in the
  day moves from ordinary, to inefficient, to evidence of bad character. Once it
  is a character judgement it no longer needs an argument, and it stops being
  examined.

## The claim to argue with

> We did not lose the nap. It was taken, for reasons that made sense to the
> people taking it, and the shame attached to it afterwards is what stopped
> anyone noticing.
