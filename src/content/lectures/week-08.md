Having spent a week on who is permitted to rest, we look at the answer industry
arrived at: buy something. The nap pod is a genuinely interesting object,
because it solves a real problem and, in solving it that way, settles a
political question quietly and in the buyer's favour.

## Outline

- **What the pod is for.** It makes daytime sleep legible to an organisation.
  Sleeping at a desk is ambiguous; sleeping in a designated capsule is
  authorised. Much of the product is that authorisation, and the furniture is
  its delivery mechanism.
- **The business case, honestly stated.** Vendors sell alertness, error
  reduction and retention, and the underlying research is real. It is also
  mostly small, mostly short-term, and mostly not conducted in the settings
  being sold to.
- **What a purchase avoids.** A pod costs money once. Shorter shifts, later
  starts and lower monitoring cost money continuously and shift control. Given
  a choice between an object and a change in schedule, institutions buy the
  object — and this is the week's central observation.
- **Counting the units.** A workplace of four hundred buys two pods. The
  arithmetic is the policy, whatever the announcement says.
- **Rest as a consumer market.** Sleep apps, weighted blankets, soundscapes,
  tracked sleep scores. Note the shift in who is responsible: rest becomes a
  personal project you may be failing at rather than a condition your employer
  arranges.
- **The exception worth taking seriously.** Aviation and some clinical settings
  have implemented rostered rest that genuinely changed practice. What was
  different was that a regulator required it. Nobody bought their way there.

## The claim to argue with

> The nap pod is not the nap returning. It is the nap being made purchasable, so
> that the schedule does not have to change.
