Until now the course has mostly concerned healthy adults on a standard day.
This week is about the people for whom the nap is least optional, and about a
pattern that holds across all three: the strength of someone's claim to daytime
sleep is a poor predictor of whether they are permitted it.

## Outline

- **Infants and children.** Polyphasic by default, consolidating gradually over
  years. Naps here are uncontroversial and institutionally provided — and the
  age at which provision stops is set by school timetables rather than by
  anything developmental.
- **Older adults.** Nighttime sleep fragments with age and daytime sleep often
  returns. The interesting part is the interpretation: the same behaviour that
  is developmentally normal in a child is read as decline in an older person,
  and sometimes medicalised.
- **Shift workers.** The clearest case in the course. Circadian misalignment is
  a measurable occupational hazard with documented consequences for error rates
  and long-term health. Rostered napping demonstrably helps.
- **And they are the least likely to get it.** Safety-critical industries
  frequently prohibit on-shift sleep outright. Week 7's explanation applies
  directly: the objection is to how it looks, not to what it does.
- **Where it was fixed, and how.** Aviation is the case worth studying, because
  controlled rest on the flight deck was regulated into existence against
  industry resistance. Not bought, not encouraged — required.
- **Who is missing from this list.** Carers, people with chronic illness or
  disability, and anyone whose fatigue is contested rather than assumed. Their
  claim is strong and their access is not a policy question anyone has taken up.

## The claim to argue with

> Across all three groups, permission to sleep in the day correlates with social
> position and not with physiological need. The nap pod cannot fix this, because
> it was never the kind of problem a purchase addresses.
